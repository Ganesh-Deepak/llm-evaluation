1 · Defining "Good" Before Writing Code

Most teams that "have evals" have a number that goes up and down, and no idea whether it measures the thing that makes users stay. The hard part of evaluation is not statistics. It is deciding, before you write a line of scoring code, what a failure is for this product, how much each kind costs, and which of them you are actually seeing. This chapter turns a vague goal ("a helpful support assistant") into a short list of measurable properties, and shows why the first step is to read outputs, with a calculation of how many you need to read.

The whole chapter in five sentences

A system is not "good"; it fails in specific ways, each with a cost, and an evaluation is a measurement of how often each costly failure happens. Write the failures down before the metrics, as a metric tree: one outcome the product exists for, the drivers that produce it, and guardrails that must not get worse. Find the failures by reading outputs, because the ones you imagine are rarely the ones you have. Reading 100 outputs reliably shows you failures above about 3%, and gives you only a rough rate for each (5 in 100 means somewhere between 2% and 11%). Then loop: change one thing, measure the failures that matter, keep or revert.

1.1 · From a product goal to a measurable property

Start with a request every team receives: "make the support assistant better". Better at what? A model evaluation (Transformers Chapter 26) can fall back on a public benchmark, because a model has no single job. A system has a job, users, and consequences, and the evaluation has to be about those. Three questions turn the request into something you can measure.

Who is the user and what are they trying to finish? A customer with a late parcel wants to know where it is and what happens next. "Helpful" means the conversation ends with that question answered, correctly, without needing a human.

What does each failure cost? Failures are not equally bad, and an evaluation that counts them equally optimises the wrong thing. For the support assistant:

FailureWhat happensCostReversible?

Correct, but curt or roboticlower satisfactionlowyes
Escalates something it could have answereda human handles a ticket the assistant could have closedlow, per ticketyes

What would you accept? Write the bar down now, before seeing a number. "No more than 1 in 500 conversations invents an order status" is a decision you can test. "As few as possible" is not, and it will be quietly renegotiated the first time a launch is near.

Optimising the average hides the expensive tail

A single "quality score from 1 to 5" averaged over conversations lets a system trade a rare catastrophic failure for a slightly friendlier tone and come out ahead. If two failures differ in cost by orders of magnitude, they need separate measurements with separate bars, never one blended number.

Properties, not vibes

Each costly failure becomes a property you can check on a single output: "every factual claim about an order matches the order database", "a refund amount is stated only if it matches the policy table", "a message mentioning fraud, a chargeback or a threat is escalated". A property is good when two careful people looking at the same output agree on whether it holds (Chapter 3 measures that agreement). "Is helpful" fails this test. "Answers the question the customer asked, with the next step stated" mostly passes it.

A team reports a 1–5 "quality" score averaged over 1,000 conversations, rising from 4.1 to 4.3 after a prompt change. Why is this weak evidence that the change is safe to ship?
1,000 conversations is too few to measure anything
Averages cannot rise by 0.2 without a real improvement
One averaged score can rise while a rare, expensive failure (an invented order status) gets more frequent
Scores on a 1–5 scale cannot be averaged at all
Blended scores hide the tail. A friendlier tone on most conversations can outweigh a doubling of a 0.3% catastrophic failure. 1,000 items is enough for many questions (Chapter 2 sizes it), and averaging an ordinal scale is crude but common; the real problem is that the score mixes failures of very different cost.
Which of these is a property two careful reviewers would most reliably agree on?
"The response is helpful"
"Every order status stated in the response matches the order record"
"The response has a good tone"
"The response is better than average"
Checkable against a record. The order-status property has a ground truth anyone can look up. "Helpful" and "good tone" are real goals, but they need definitions and examples before people agree on them (Chapter 3), and "better than average" depends on what else was seen.
Why write the acceptance bar ("at most 1 in 500 conversations invents an order status") before seeing any results?
Because the statistics only work with a pre-registered threshold
Because the bar determines the model you should use
Because a bar is required by every CI system
Because a bar set after seeing the number tends to move to wherever the number landed
To keep yourself honest. Pre-registration is also what makes significance tests valid (Chapter 6), but the main reason is human: deadlines renegotiate unwritten bars. The bar does not choose the model, and CI gates (Chapter 16) need a threshold but not this particular one.

1.2 · Metric trees: an outcome, its drivers, its guardrails

Online experimentation settled long ago how to organise many metrics so that they do not contradict each other (Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020). Use three layers.

The outcome (sometimes called the north-star or overall evaluation criterion) is the one thing the product exists for, measured over many users and often slowly: support tickets resolved without a human, with no reopen within 7 days. You rarely evaluate it offline, because it needs real users and time.

The drivers are properties of individual outputs that cause the outcome, and that you can measure offline on a golden set: the answer is correct; the policy stated matches the policy table; the order facts match the record; the next step is stated; an escalation happens when, and only when, it should.

The guardrails are things that must not get worse while the drivers improve: invented facts, unsafe content, cost per conversation, p95 latency, the rate of unneeded escalations.

The tree does two jobs. It tells you what a change is allowed to trade: a prompt that raises "next step stated" by 4 points while raising cost per conversation by 30% may or may not be worth it, but it is a decision someone makes on purpose, not a side effect nobody saw. And it tells you which metric is lying: if every driver improves and the outcome does not move online, a driver is not actually a cause, or something unmeasured got worse. That gap between offline drivers and the online outcome is the subject of Chapter 17.

A driver that is easy to measure is not automatically a driver

Response length, number of citations, "uses the customer's name": all easy to count, and all easy to raise without resolving a single extra ticket. A driver earns its place by a plausible causal story and, eventually, by moving together with the outcome in online data. Until then it is a hypothesis, and the tree should say so.

1.3 · Error analysis first: read the outputs

The single most common mistake is to choose metrics before looking at what the system actually does. The failures you imagine in a design document are rarely the ones you have. The cure is old and unglamorous: take a sample of real outputs, read them, write down what went wrong in your own words, then group the notes into categories. Qualitative researchers call the two steps open coding (free-text notes per item) and axial coding (grouping the notes into a small set of categories) (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). Practitioners building LLM products arrived at the same procedure (Husain, 2024), and Shankar and colleagues found that people only discover their real evaluation criteria while grading outputs: criteria drift as you read (Shankar et al., 2024). You cannot write the rubric first.

How many outputs is enough?

There is a clean answer to the question everyone asks. Suppose a failure mode occurs independently in a fraction p of outputs. The chance that n outputs contain none of it is (1 − p)n, so the chance you see it at least once is:

P(seen) = 1 − (1 − p)n   ⇒   n ≥ ln(1 − c) / ln(1 − p) reads needed to see a failure mode of frequency p with confidence c

So the folk rule "read 100 outputs" has a precise meaning: it reliably surfaces every failure mode above about 3%, and gives a coin-flip-or-better chance at modes around 1%. Thirty outputs, which is what most people read before getting bored, misses a 3% failure mode four times in ten. Modes rarer than 1% will not show up in casual reading at all; finding them is what targeted sampling (Chapter 2) and production monitoring (Chapter 17) are for.

What a count of failures actually tells you

Error analysis also gives you rough rates. Five failures in 100 outputs says the rate is 5%, give or take a lot. How much? The interval taught in most textbooks, p̂ ± 1.96·√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) (the Wald interval), behaves badly exactly where error analysis lives, at small counts. For 1 failure in 100 it gives [−1.0%, 3.0%], a negative rate; for 0 in 100 it gives [0, 0], certainty from no data. The Wilson score interval (Wilson, 1927) fixes both:

centre = (p̂ + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n),    half-width = z·√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n²) / (1 + z²/n) Wilson score interval; z = 1.96 for 95%

Read the 5-in-100 row carefully: the honest statement is "somewhere between 2% and 11%". That is enough to rank failure modes by rough size and to decide what to work on. It is nowhere near enough to tell whether a fix moved a rate from 5% to 4%. Error analysis finds and sizes problems; measuring changes needs the larger, structured sets of Chapters 2 and 6.

Where the 0-in-100 upper bound of 3.7% comes from

With p̂ = 0 the Wilson upper bound is z²/(n + z²) = 3.84/103.84 = 3.7%. A simpler rule for zero observed failures is the rule of three: the 95% upper bound is about 3/n (Chapter 14 derives it). Both say the same thing: seeing nothing in 100 reads rules out only failure modes above a few percent.

Error analysis sizes problems; it does not measure changes. A 5→3 difference is well within chance at n = 100. "No difference" is equally unsupported: the data cannot distinguish the two. 1.4 · The eval-driven development loop

Once you know what fails, the work becomes a loop, and the loop is the reason to build evaluations at all:

The loop
1
Read and categorise. Sample real outputs (100 is a good first batch), open-code them, group the notes into failure modes, count each, attach a Wilson interval.
2
Pick one failure mode, the most costly per occurrence times its rate, not the most frequent.
3
Write a check for it: code if possible (a regex, a database lookup, a schema), a judge model if not (Chapters 9 and 10). Run the check on the items you already labelled by hand, and make sure it agrees with you.
4
Change one thing (prompt, retrieval, model, tool) and run the check on enough items to see the change you care about (Chapter 6 computes "enough").
5
Keep or revert, and look at the guardrails before keeping. Add the items that failed to the golden set, so the failure cannot quietly return (Chapter 16).

Two properties make the loop work. It is cheap to run: if measuring a change takes a day, people stop measuring. And the checks are trusted: a check that disagrees with a human on 20% of items will be ignored the first time it blocks something someone wants. Part III is about earning that trust for model-graded checks.

How teams do this in 2026

The practice that has become standard among teams shipping LLM features (and is taught widely, for example in Husain and Shankar's evals course) is exactly this loop: error analysis by hand on traces first, a small number of binary pass/fail checks per failure mode rather than 1–10 scores, LLM judges only for properties code cannot check, and judges validated against human labels before they are trusted. The tooling has changed every year; the loop has not.

Measuring what you can, instead of what matters

The failure mode that is easiest to check by code is rarely the most costly. Teams build a beautiful JSON-validity check (a 0.5% problem) and leave "states a wrong refund policy" (a 6% problem with a real cost) to anecdote, because the second needs a policy table and a judge. Pick by cost × rate, then find a way to measure it.

Error analysis finds: invalid JSON in 9% of outputs (caught and retried automatically, costing a little latency), and a wrong refund amount in 4% (leading to complaints). Which should the loop take first?
Invalid JSON, because it is more frequent
Invalid JSON, because it is easier to check
Neither: fix the model first
The wrong refund amount, because cost per occurrence times rate is far higher
Cost × rate, not rate. The JSON failure is already mitigated by a retry and costs little. A wrong refund amount reaches the customer. Ease of checking matters for how you build the check, not for which failure matters.
Your new judge for "states a wrong refund policy" agrees with your own hand labels on only 70% of the 100 items you coded. What next?
Use it anyway: 70% is better than chance
Read the disagreements: either the judge's instructions are unclear or your own definition drifted while coding
Replace the judge with a larger model
Average the judge with your labels
Disagreements are data. Shankar et al. found that criteria drift during grading, so some disagreements are your definition changing. A larger model may help, but only once you know what it is getting wrong. Chapter 10 makes "agrees on 70%" precise as false-positive and false-negative rates.
Why add failing items to the golden set after a fix?
So a later change that brings the failure back is caught
To raise the golden set's pass rate
Because golden sets must only contain failures
To make the next error analysis unnecessary
Regression protection. Fixed bugs come back in LLM systems all the time: a prompt edit, a model upgrade, a retrieval change. A golden set of only failures would be biased (Chapter 2 balances it), and error analysis never stops, because new failure modes appear as traffic changes.

1.5 · Worked example: defining "good" for a support assistant

Put it together on a concrete system. A retailer's support assistant answers questions about orders, returns, billing and accounts, with tools that look up orders and the refund-policy table. The team plans a prompt change and asks whether the assistant is "good enough to expand".

Worked example — from "good enough" to four measurable properties
1
Read 100 conversations. In this example the system's true failure rates are 6% wrong policy, 3% invented order status, 2% missed escalation and 8% poor tone (the simulation in evalkit.basics.simulate_failure_reading, seed 1). The 100 conversations the team reads contain 9 wrong-policy answers, 7 invented statuses, 0 missed escalations and 13 tone problems.
2
Attach intervals. Wilson 95%: wrong policy [4.8%, 16.2%]; invented status [3.4%, 13.7%]; missed escalation [0.0%, 3.7%]; tone [7.8%, 21.0%]. Every point estimate is off, and one interval misses: the true invented-status rate, 3%, sits just below [3.4%, 13.7%] because this sample happened to contain an unusually high 7 of them. A 95% interval misses about one time in twenty, and with four of them on one page, seeing a miss is unremarkable. That is what n = 100 gives you.
3
Notice what reading missed. The 2% escalation failure did not appear at all, which happens 13.3% of the time (0.98100). It is also the most costly failure on the list. Reading alone would have declared it absent. The fix is to sample on purpose: pull every conversation that mentions fraud, chargebacks or threats and read those (Chapter 2).
4
Write the properties and bars. Policy matches the table (bar: ≤ 2%); order facts match the record (≤ 0.2%, i.e. 1 in 500); escalation recall on the risk set (≥ 99%); tone judged acceptable (≥ 90%). Note the bars differ by an order of magnitude, because the costs do.
5
Plan the checks. Order facts: code (compare against the order API). Policy: code plus a lookup table where amounts are stated; a judge for the rest. Escalation: a labelled risk set and a check on the tool call. Tone: a judge, validated against 100 hand labels (Chapter 10).

Notice how much of this happened before any metric code existed, and how much it changed the plan. The original request ("is it good enough?") had no answer. The rewritten one ("is order-fact accuracy at least 99.8% and escalation recall at least 99% on the risk set?") does, and Chapter 2 will tell the team how many items each answer needs: the 1-in-500 bar alone needs thousands.

In the worked example, why did reading 100 random conversations miss the escalation failure?
The reviewers were careless
The simulation had a bug
At 2%, zero occurrences in 100 reads happens about 13% of the time, and risky conversations are rare in random traffic
Escalation failures cannot be seen in transcripts
Rare and costly is the worst combination for random sampling. 0.98100 = 0.133. The fix is not to read more random conversations but to sample the risky ones on purpose.
Why do the four bars differ by orders of magnitude (≤ 2%, ≤ 0.2%, ≥ 99%, ≥ 90%)?
Because the failures differ in cost by orders of magnitude
Because rarer failures are easier to fix
Because the golden set has different sizes per failure
Because judges are less accurate than code
Bars follow cost. An invented order status cannot be undone; a curt reply can. The golden-set sizes follow from the bars (Chapter 2), not the other way round.
The tone check uses a judge model. What must happen before its number is trusted?
It must use the largest available model
It must output a 1–10 score rather than pass/fail
It must be run twice and averaged
Its verdicts must be compared with human labels on the same items, and its error rates measured
A judge is a measuring instrument and needs calibrating. Chapter 10 measures its false-positive and false-negative rates and corrects the estimate for them. Size helps but proves nothing; binary verdicts are usually more reliable than fine-grained scores; repeating a biased judge repeats its bias.

1.6 · The mental model to keep

Failures, costs, bars, then metrics

An evaluation answers "how often does each costly failure happen?", so it starts with a list of failures and their costs, not a metric. Find the failures by reading outputs: 100 reads surface everything above about 3% and size each roughly. Rare, costly failures need targeted samples, not more random reading. Write each bar before seeing a number. Then run the loop: one failure mode, one check you trust, one change, keep or revert, and the guardrails watched throughout.

1.7 · Exercises

1.1Write the metric tree for a product you knowessential

Pick an LLM feature you use or build (code review comments, meeting summaries, a search answer box). Write its outcome, three to five drivers that are properties of a single output, and three guardrails. For each driver, say whether code, a database lookup or a judge would check it, and what a failure costs.

What a good answer looks like

For meeting summaries: outcome = "summaries read and not edited, attendees do not ask what was decided". Drivers: every decision stated in the meeting appears (judge against a transcript, or human labels on a sample); every action item has an owner (code: a parse of the "Actions" section); no statement contradicts the transcript (judge). Guardrails: length, cost, any personal data from private chat included (code). The test of a good tree: someone else could build the checks from your descriptions without asking you what you meant.

1.2How many reads?essential

(a) How many outputs must you read to see a 0.5% failure mode at least once with 95% probability? With 80%? (b) You have budget to read 200. What is the rarest failure mode you will see with 95% probability? (c) Why might the real numbers be worse than these, for failures that cluster in particular users or topics?

Solution

(a) ln 0.05 / ln 0.995 = 597.6, so 598; ln 0.2 / ln 0.995 = 321.1, so 322. (b) Solve 1 − (1 − p)200 = 0.95: p = 1 − 0.051/200 = 1.49%. (c) The formula assumes every output fails independently with the same probability. If a failure happens only for one product line that makes up 3% of traffic (at a 30% rate there), a random sample sees it far less evenly than the formula suggests, and a sample drawn from one day may contain none of that product line at all. Stratify the sample (Chapter 2).

1.3Implement Wilson and break Waldimplementation

Implement the Wilson interval. Then simulate: for a true rate of 2% and n = 100, draw 10,000 samples, compute the Wald and Wilson 95% intervals for each, and report how often each contains 2%. Predict first: which one undercovers, and by roughly how much?

Solution

Wald undercovers badly at small p·n: most of its misses come from samples with 0 or 1 failures, where it gives a zero-width or negative interval. In the companion notebook (10,000 draws, seed 0) the Wald coverage at p = 2%, n = 100 is 86.5%, while Wilson's is 94.5%, close to the nominal 95%. The lesson: at small counts the interval formula matters as much as the data.

1.4Predict, then measure: what reading missespredict then measure

Using simulate_failure_reading with the worked example's rates, run 1,000 simulated reads of 100 conversations each. Before running: in what fraction will the 2% escalation failure be missing entirely? In what fraction will the 3% invented-status failure look larger than the 6% wrong-policy failure? Then measure both.

Solution

Missing entirely: 0.98100 = 13.3% of runs. Ranked wrongly (3% mode counted above the 6% mode): 12.0% of runs, with a further 8.3% tied (the notebook's 1,000 runs give 13.5% for the missing escalation, against the 13.3% predicted). Both effects are why the rough rates from error analysis decide what to look at next, never what to report.

1.5The cost of the barsystems estimate

The worked example sets "invented order status ≤ 1 in 500". (a) If a golden set shows zero invented statuses, how many items must it have before the 95% upper bound is below 0.2%? Use the rule of three. (b) Each item costs about 1,200 input and 300 output tokens through the assistant, plus a 2,000-token judge call; at $3 per million input and $15 per million output tokens for both, what does one evaluation run cost? (c) What does that suggest about running this check on every commit?

Solution

(a) 3/n < 0.002 ⇒ n > 1,500 items, with zero failures among them. (b) Per item: assistant 1,200 × $3/M + 300 × $15/M = $0.0081; judge 2,000 × $3/M plus a short verdict (say 50 tokens, $0.00075) ≈ $0.0068; total ≈ $0.015, so about $22 per 1,500-item run. (c) Cheap enough nightly; on every commit it adds up and slows the loop, which is why Chapter 16 splits checks into tiers. This check is mostly code (compare with the order record), so the judge cost can be dropped entirely.
