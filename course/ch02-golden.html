2 · Golden Sets: Building, Sampling, Maintaining

A golden set is the fixed collection of inputs (and, where possible, expected outputs) that every version of your system is measured on. It is the most valuable artefact an AI team owns, and it is usually built badly: a few dozen examples someone typed in on day one, never weighted, never refreshed, and tuned against so often that its numbers mean nothing. This chapter builds one properly. The hard parts are where the items come from, how many you need (the answer comes from the error bar you can tolerate), and how to keep the set honest once people start optimising against it.

The whole chapter in five sentences

Sample items from real traffic, stratified by the slices that behave differently (intent, length, language, difficulty), so the set represents what users send. Add hard and rare cases on purpose, label them as such, and weight them back to their traffic share when you report an overall number. Size the set from the error bar you need: ±z·√(p(1−p)/n) means ±7 points at 200 items and ±2 points at 2,401, and every slice you report separately needs its own n. Version it, with provenance for every item, because traffic and products drift. Keep a held-out test split: picking the best of 20 equally good prompts on the same 200 items inflates the winner by about 6 points.

2.1 · Sampling from real traffic, stratified

The first source of items is production traffic, or the closest thing to it you have (beta users, internal dogfooding, a log from a previous system). Items you write yourself are useful, but they reflect what you imagine users do, which Chapter 1 showed is a poor guide. A golden set is an estimate of how the system behaves on the traffic it will actually see, so it should be a sample of that traffic.

A simple random sample is the honest default: every logged request equally likely to be chosen. Its weakness is small slices. If account questions are 2% of traffic, a random 400-item set contains about 8 of them, and any number you report for that slice is noise. Stratified sampling fixes this: split traffic into strata that behave differently (here, by intent), then sample a chosen number from each.

How many from each? Three standard rules, each optimal for a different goal:

RuleItems in stratum hBest for
Proportionalnh ∝ Nh (its share of traffic)a set that looks like traffic; simple overall numbers
Neymannh ∝ Nh · σh (share × variability)the smallest error on the overall traffic-weighted rate
Equaln / H eachreporting every slice with the same precision
Worked example — 400 items across four intents
1
The traffic. In a month of support logs: billing 6,000 conversations, shipping 3,000, returns 800, account 200. From error analysis (Chapter 1) the pass rates are roughly 90% for billing and shipping, so σ = √(0.9 × 0.1) = 0.30, around 70% for returns (σ ≈ 0.45), and around 50% for account questions (σ = 0.50).
2
Proportional gives billing 240, shipping 120, returns 32, account 8. The standard error of the traffic-weighted pass rate is 1.60 points, but the account slice is measured on 8 items.
3
Neyman shifts items toward the variable slices: 228, 114, 45, 13. The overall standard error barely moves (1.58 points): Neyman's gain is small when strata differ little in variability.
4
Equal gives 100 each. The overall standard error rises to 2.05 points, because small strata are over-represented and must be down-weighted, but every slice can now be reported with ±6 to ±10 points instead of ±35.

There is no free lunch: the allocation that is best for the overall number is poor for small slices, and the reverse. Decide which numbers you will report, then allocate. A common compromise is proportional plus a floor: proportional allocation, but no slice below some minimum (say 50).

SE2(overall) = Σh Wh2 · σh2 / nh,    Wh = Nh / N the variance of a stratified mean: each slice's variance, weighted by the square of its traffic share
Sampling by time slices hides seasonality

"The last 500 conversations" is not a random sample of traffic; it is a sample of one afternoon. Returns spike after holidays, billing questions at month end, and a product launch changes everything. Sample across the whole period you care about, or stratify by week.

A 400-item golden set sampled at random from traffic contains 8 account questions. Why is a separately reported "account pass rate" from it nearly useless?

Small n, huge interval. 1.96·√(0.5·0.5/8) = 0.35. Random sampling is unbiased, it just gives small slices few items; stratified sampling with a floor would give that slice as many as you choose.
When does Neyman allocation beat proportional allocation by a lot?
When strata differ greatly in variability (some near 50% pass, some near 99%)
When all strata have the same pass rate
When the golden set is very large
Never: they always give the same error
Neyman weights by σh. If every stratum has the same σ, Neyman reduces to proportional. Its advantage grows with the spread of σ: a stratum at 99% (σ = 0.1) needs far fewer items than one at 50% (σ = 0.5). In the worked example the σs ranged only from 0.30 to 0.50, so the gain was small.
Equal allocation (100 per intent) raised the overall standard error from 1.60 to 2.05 points. Why?
Equal allocation is biased
It uses fewer items in total
The pass rates changed
The big strata, which carry most of the traffic weight, now have far fewer items
Weight times variance over n. Billing carries W = 0.6 of the weight but now has 100 items instead of 240, so its term W²σ²/n grows. Equal allocation is not biased (as long as you weight back), and it uses the same 400 items.

2.2 · Hard cases on purpose, weighted back

Random and stratified samples represent traffic. They do not, by themselves, contain the rare, costly cases Chapter 1 found: fraud reports, legal threats, prompt injection attempts, the 0.2% of conversations in a language the system barely supports. Add them deliberately, in their own targeted slices, from three sources:

  • Filters over logs: every conversation mentioning "chargeback", "lawyer", "police"; every one longer than 40 turns; every one where a human took over.
  • Past incidents: every failure that reached a customer, and every bug report, becomes an item, so it can never quietly return.
  • Written adversarial cases: boundary conditions the system must handle (a refund request one day past the policy window), and attacks (Chapter 14 and the Security course).

Mark every item with its source. The mistake is to pour targeted items into the same pool and report one unweighted pass rate: the number then describes neither traffic nor the hard cases.

Worked example — reporting with an over-sampled risk slice
1
Risky conversations are 5% of traffic. The golden set has 300 normal items (276 pass, 92%) and 300 risky items (240 pass, 80%).
2
Unweighted: (276 + 240) / 600 = 86.0%. This is not the pass rate of anything users experience: risky cases are 50% of the set but 5% of traffic.
3
Weighted back to traffic: 0.95 × 92% + 0.05 × 80% = 91.4%, with a standard error of √(0.95²·0.92·0.08/300 + 0.05²·0.80·0.20/300) = 1.49 points.
4
Report three numbers: the traffic-weighted 91.4% ± 2.9 (95%), the normal slice and the risky slice separately. The risky slice gets its own bar (Chapter 1): 80% on fraud reports is a launch blocker even though the weighted number looks fine.
Once over-sampled, always weighted

Any change to the mix of slices changes an unweighted pass rate even if the system did not change. Add 100 hard items to fix a blind spot and the headline number drops 3 points; someone files a bug about the "regression". Report traffic-weighted numbers and per-slice numbers, never the raw pool average.

Regression items. They go in a targeted slice (they are not representative of traffic), usually with a near-100% bar, and they make the set stronger every time something goes wrong.
In the worked example, why is the weighted standard error (1.49 points) close to the normal slice's own standard error (1.57 points)?
The normal slice carries 95% of the weight, so its variance dominates the sum

W² weighting. The risky slice contributes 0.05² × its variance, which is tiny. The weighted SE is 0.95 × 1.57 ≈ 1.49 plus a negligible risky-slice term. A slice with little traffic barely moves the overall number, which is exactly why it needs its own report.

2.3 · Size the set from the error bar you can tolerate

"How many items do we need?" has a direct answer once you say how precise the number must be. For a pass rate p measured on n independent items, the 95% interval is approximately ±1.96·√(p(1 − p)/n). Invert it for the size that gives a margin m:

n = z² · p(1 − p) / m² items for a ±m interval on a pass rate near p; z = 1.96 for 95%
margin (95%)n at p = 50%n at p = 80%n at p = 90%
±1 point9,6046,1473,458
±2 points2,4011,537865
±3 points1,068683385
±5 points385246139
±7 points19612671

Three facts follow. Precision costs quadratically: halving the interval needs four times the items. Rates near 50% are the most expensive to measure, because p(1 − p) is largest there. And a 200-item golden set measures a pass rate to about ±7 points, which is fine for "is this broken?" and useless for "did my prompt change help by 2 points?". Comparisons between two versions are a different and cheaper calculation, because both versions see the same items (Chapter 6).

Slices, rare bars, and the real total

The table is per number. If you report six slices at ±5 points each, you need about 6 × 246 items (at an 80% pass rate), not 246. And very strict bars on rare failures are expensive in a different way: to show "at most 1 in 500" with 95% confidence when you observe zero failures, the rule of three (Chapter 14) needs n > 3/0.002 = 1,500 items, every one of which must pass. Add up what each reported number needs before choosing a size; it is usually larger than anyone expected, and the right response is often to report fewer numbers.

Why the items must be independent

The formula assumes each item is an independent draw. Five paraphrases of one question, or twenty turns from one conversation, are not five or twenty items' worth of information: they rise and fall together. Chapter 5 measures how much this shrinks the effective size (the design effect). Count independent scenarios, not rows.

Correlated rows count for less. If turns were independent, 1,000; if every turn in a conversation passed or failed together, 200. Real data is in between, and Chapter 5 computes where from the within-conversation correlation.

2.4 · Versioning, provenance, and refresh

A golden set is code. It changes, and every number must say which version it was computed on. Store it as a file in version control (JSON Lines works well), one item per line, and give every item enough metadata to answer the questions people will ask about it later:

{"id": "gs-000417",
 "input": {"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "I was charged twice for order 88213"}]},
 "slice": "billing",
 "source": "traffic-sample-2026-10",        # or "incident-INC-2231", "written-adversarial"
 "expected": {"escalate": false, "must_mention": ["refund the duplicate charge"]},
 "label_by": ["ann-03", "ann-07"], "agreement": "unanimous",
 "added": "2026-10-14", "version": "v7",
 "notes": "customer-facing amount must match the order record"}

Why provenance matters: when a pass rate drops after an edit to the set, you need to know which items were added or changed; when a legal review asks where an item came from, "someone typed it" is a bad answer; and when an item turns out to be mislabelled, you need to find the others the same annotator labelled.

Golden sets rot

Traffic drifts: new products, new policies, new user populations, new ways of attacking the system. A set built in January measures January. Two practices keep it current. Refresh on a schedule: every month or quarter, sample new traffic into a fresh slice and compare the system's pass rate on it with the old set; a gap means the old set no longer represents traffic. Retire items deliberately: when a policy changes, the items that encode the old policy are now wrong, and keeping them silently rewards the old behaviour. Never edit an item in place; retire it and add the corrected one, so old numbers stay reproducible.

Current practice (2026)

Teams increasingly build golden sets directly from production traces (the trace store from the Production AI course), with every item linked to the trace it came from, and refresh them continuously from flagged conversations and user-reported failures. The statistical principles here do not change with the tooling: sample deliberately, weight back, size from the margin, version everything.

2.5 · Held-out discipline: the set you tune on is not the set you report

The fastest way to make a golden set meaningless is to use it for everything: tune prompts on it, pick models on it, choose thresholds on it, then report its pass rate as the system's quality. Every choice made by looking at a set makes the set look better than the system is, even when nobody cheats.

Here is the effect in its purest form. Suppose you try K prompt variants that are all exactly equally good (true pass rate 70%) and keep whichever scores highest on the same 200 items. Each measured score is the truth plus noise with standard deviation √(0.7·0.3/200) = 3.24 points. The winner is the one with the largest noise, and the expected largest of K standard normal draws is known:

variants tried, KE[max of K standard normals]expected inflation of the winner (n = 200, p = 70%)
20.564+1.8 points
51.163+3.8 points
101.539+5.0 points
201.867+6.1 points
502.249+7.3 points

Simulating it directly (2,000 trials of 20 equally good variants on 200 items) gives a measured inflation of 5.9 points, close to the 6.1 predicted (the small gap is because pass rates on 200 items are discrete). Six points of "improvement" from selection alone is larger than most real prompt improvements. And real tuning is worse than this pure case, because people also tweak the prompt in response to the specific items it fails.

The fix is a split, used in a fixed order

Development split (60–80% of items): tune, inspect, iterate as much as you like. Test split (the rest): run once per decision, never inspect individual failures, never tune against. When the test split has been used for many decisions, it has been tuned against too: retire it into development and draw a fresh test split from new traffic.

A team tried 30 prompt variants on a 150-item set and reports the best one as "+5 points over baseline". What is the most important missing evidence?
The winner's score on a held-out set that played no part in the selection
The prompt text of all 30 variants
The baseline's score on the same 150 items
The judge model used
Selection inflates the winner. With 30 equally good variants on 150 items, the expected inflation is around 2·σ, several points: possibly all of the +5. Only an untouched held-out set can tell. The baseline on the same items is necessary too, but it does not remove the selection effect.
Why does the expected inflation grow with K but slowly (1.8 points at K = 2, only 7.3 at K = 50)?
Because later variants are better
Because the noise shrinks as K grows
The expected maximum of K normal draws grows roughly like √(2 ln K)
Because pass rates are bounded at 100%
Extreme-value growth is logarithmic. Even 50 variants push the expected maximum only to 2.25σ. That slowness is deceptive: with real effects small, a few σ of inflation is enough to fake a result.
Which practice keeps a test split honest the longest?
Reading the test failures after each run to understand them
Running it rarely, only for decisions, and never inspecting or tuning against individual items
Making it as large as the development split
Re-sampling it from the development split every week
Every look leaks. Reading failures turns the test split into a development split. Size helps precision but not leakage, and re-drawing from development data defeats the purpose.

2.6 · The mental model to keep

A golden set is a sample, a ruler, and a secret

A sample: drawn from real traffic, stratified, with hard slices added on purpose and weighted back. A ruler: its size sets its resolution, ±7 points at 200 items, ±2 at 2,401, separately for every number you report. A secret: the part you report on is never tuned against, because choosing the best of K variants on the same items inflates the winner by about √(2 ln K) standard errors. Version it like code, and refresh it before it stops describing your traffic.

Compute the proportional, Neyman and equal allocations, the overall standard error under each, and the per-language 95% margins. Which would you choose, and why?

Solution

σ: 0.271, 0.357, 0.433. Proportional: 510/60/30 (overall SE 1.19 points); Neyman (∝ N·σ): 481/74/45 (1.18); equal: 200/200/200 (1.66). Per-language 95% margins under proportional: English ±2.4, Spanish ±9.0, German ±15.5 on 30 items, too wide to act on. A good choice is proportional with a floor: 400/100/100 costs little overall precision (SE 1.23 points) and gives Spanish ±7.0 and German ±8.5.

2.2Weight it backessential

A golden set has 500 general items (pass 88%), 200 long-document items (pass 70%, 8% of traffic) and 100 prompt-injection items (pass 60%, 0.5% of traffic). General traffic is the remaining 91.5%. Compute the unweighted pool rate, the traffic-weighted rate and its 95% interval. What would you put in the release notes?

Solution

Unweighted: (440 + 140 + 60)/800 = 80.0%. Weighted: 0.915 × 0.88 + 0.08 × 0.70 + 0.005 × 0.60 = 86.4%. SE = √(0.915²·0.88·0.12/500 + 0.08²·0.7·0.3/200 + 0.005²·0.6·0.4/100) = 1.35 points, so ±2.7. Release notes: "86% on traffic-weighted tasks (±3); long documents 70%; prompt-injection resistance 60% (below the 95% bar: blocker)". The injection slice barely affects the overall number and is the most important line.

Solution

2.4Price the setsystems estimate

You need the overall rate to ±2 points (pass ≈ 85%), plus five slices to ±5 points each, plus the 1-in-500 invented-status bar from Chapter 1. Labelling costs $1.50 per normal item and $6 per expert-labelled slice item. How many items, and what does labelling cost? Which requirement dominates?

Solution

Overall ±2 at 85%: 1.96²·0.85·0.15/0.0004 = 1,225 items. Slices ±5 at 85%: 196 each, 980 total (some can overlap with the overall sample if slices are a stratification of it). The 1-in-500 bar: 1,500 items with zero failures, but that check is code against the order record, so those items need no human label. Labelling roughly 1,225 × $1.50 + 980 × $6 ≈ $7,700. The slice requirement dominates the cost, which argues for fewer, better-chosen slices.

2.6Detect a stale setstretch

Your system scores 90% on the January golden set. A fresh sample of 300 items from October traffic scores 82%. Is the system getting worse, or is the January set stale? Design one measurement that tells the two apart.

Solution

Run the January version of the system on the October sample. If it also scores about 82%, traffic changed and the old set is stale (both versions are equally good on what users now send). If it scores about 90%, the system regressed. This is the same trick as a paired comparison: hold the items fixed and vary only the thing you are asking about.
