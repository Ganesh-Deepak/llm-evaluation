3 · Labels and Agreement

Every evaluation rests on labels: a person decided this output passes and that one fails, and everything downstream (pass rates, judges, significance tests) inherits whatever noise and bias went into those decisions. If two careful people label the same outputs and disagree on one in five, no metric built on their labels can be more precise than that. This chapter writes guidelines people can follow, measures agreement properly (raw percentage agreement flatters you, sometimes badly), and treats disagreement as information about the task rather than an annoyance to average away.

The whole chapter in five sentences

A labelling guideline is a definition plus examples plus counterexamples, and most disagreement comes from cases it does not cover. Raw agreement is inflated by luck: two reviewers who pass 80% of everything agree on 68% of items by chance alone, so measure agreement with kappa, (po − pe)/(1 − pe). Kappa falls when one label is rare even at high raw agreement (92% agreement can be κ = 0.29), so always report the raw agreement and the label balance beside it. For more than two raters or missing labels use Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha, and read the pairwise kappas to find the rater who drifted. When agreement stays low after the guideline is fixed, the property may be genuinely subjective: measure the distribution of opinions, do not force a single gold label.

3.1 · Guidelines that work

A label is only as reproducible as the instructions behind it. "Mark the response as failing if it is wrong or unhelpful" will produce labels that mostly reflect each reviewer's mood. A guideline that produces consistent labels has four parts.

A definition in terms of the output, not the reviewer's reaction. "Fail if any stated refund amount, date or order status differs from the order record" can be checked. "Fail if it would frustrate the customer" asks the reviewer to imagine a person.

Positive examples: three to five outputs that clearly pass, with one sentence each on why. Negative examples: the same for clear failures. Boundary cases, the most valuable part: outputs that sit near the line, with the decision and the reason. "States the right refund amount but says it arrives in 3 days when policy says 5 to 7: fail, because the timeline is a stated fact." Reviewers disagree almost entirely on boundary cases, so every disagreement you resolve becomes a new boundary example.

An escalation rule: what to do when unsure. "Mark unsure and write one sentence" is far better than a forced guess, because unsure items are exactly the ones that improve the guideline. Count them; a guideline with 15% unsure is not finished.

Binary first

1-to-5 scales feel richer, but reviewers disagree about what separates a 3 from a 4, and those disagreements are mostly noise. Practitioners and the judge literature agree: per failure mode, ask a binary pass/fail question with a written definition. If you need degrees, use several binary questions ("states the policy correctly", "states the next step", "acceptable tone") rather than one scale.

3.2 · Agreement, corrected for chance

Two reviewers, A and B, label the same 100 outputs as pass or fail:

B: passB: failtotal
A: pass701080

They agree on 70 + 15 = 85 items: observed agreement po = 0.85. That sounds good, but some of it is luck. A passes 80% of outputs and B passes 75%. If each labelled at random with those rates, they would agree on both-pass with probability 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60 and on both-fail with 0.20 × 0.25 = 0.05: chance agreement pe = 0.65. Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) measures how much of the possible improvement over chance they achieved:

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe) = (0.85 − 0.65) / (1 − 0.65) = 0.571 0 = no better than chance, 1 = perfect, negative = worse than chance

So "85% agreement" is really "57% of the way from chance to perfect". The common descriptive scale (Landis and Koch, 1977) calls 0.41–0.60 "moderate" and 0.61–0.80 "substantial"; Krippendorff (2004) recommends treating α ≥ 0.80 as reliable and 0.67–0.80 as tentative. Use them as rough guides only. What matters is whether the labels are precise enough for the decision they feed, and Chapter 10 makes that quantitative.

The kappa paradox: high agreement, low kappa

Now take a task where almost everything passes. Two reviewers agree on 92 of 100 items: both pass 90, both fail 2, and they split 4 and 4. Raw agreement is 92%, but each passes 94% of items, so chance agreement is 0.94² + 0.06² = 0.887, and κ = (0.92 − 0.887)/(1 − 0.887) = 0.29. They agreed on only 2 of the 10 items either of them failed. This is the kappa paradox (Feinstein and Cicchetti, 1990): when one label is rare, chance agreement is high and kappa is low, even though the reviewers agree on almost everything.

The paradox is not a flaw to explain away; it is kappa doing its job. For a rare failure mode, what you care about is whether reviewers agree on the failures, and here they mostly do not: of 10 items flagged by anyone, they agree on 2. Report three things together and the picture is never misleading: raw agreement, kappa, and the label balance (and, for rare labels, agreement among the items either reviewer flagged).

3.3 · Many raters, missing labels: Fleiss and Krippendorff

Cohen's kappa is for exactly two raters who labelled the same items. Real labelling has three raters per item, or a pool of ten raters where each item gets two or three of them, or some items labelled by only one person.

Fleiss' kappa (Fleiss, 1971) handles any fixed number of raters per item. For each item, the agreement is the fraction of rater pairs that agree, Pi = (Σj nij² − r)/(r(r − 1)) for r raters and nij of them choosing category j. The chance level is Σj pj², from the overall category shares. Then κ = (P̄ − Pe)/(1 − Pe), the same shape as before.

Krippendorff's alpha (Krippendorff, 2004) is the most general: any number of raters, missing labels, and nominal, ordinal or interval data. It counts, over all items, how often pairs of labels on the same item disagree (Do), compares that with how often labels drawn at random from the whole pool would disagree (De), and reports α = 1 − Do/De. Our implementation reproduces the worked example in Krippendorff's own paper (α = 0.743 for 4 coders, 12 items, nominal data, with gaps), and Fleiss' kappa reproduces the standard textbook example (κ = 0.210 for 14 raters and 5 categories); both are asserted in the tests.

3.4 · When agreement stays low

You fixed the guideline, added boundary cases, ran a calibration session, and agreement is still around 0.4. There are three explanations, and they call for different responses.

The task is ambiguous as specified. "Is this summary complete?" depends on what the reader needed. Narrow it: "does the summary mention every decision recorded in the meeting's action log?" turns a judgement into a check.

The guideline is still missing a rule. Collect every disagreement, group them, and you will often find two or three recurring patterns. Each becomes a boundary example. This loop typically takes agreement from moderate to substantial in two or three rounds.

The property is genuinely subjective. "Is this joke funny?" or "is this tone too formal?" have no single right answer: reasonable people differ, consistently. Forcing a gold label by majority vote throws away real information about how users will react. Aroyo and Welty (2015) argued for keeping the distribution of labels instead ("CrowdTruth"): an output that 3 of 5 raters find too formal is a different item from one that 5 of 5 do, and the system should be measured on how often it produces outputs most users dislike, not on matching one rater's taste.

Adjudication: turning labels into gold

For properties that do have a right answer, convert several labels into one gold label. Three common rules: majority vote (cheap; ties go to a fourth rater or an expert), expert adjudication of every disagreement (best for high-stakes labels, and it produces boundary examples as a by-product), and model-based aggregation such as Dawid–Skene (1979), which estimates each rater's error rates and weights their votes accordingly. Whatever you use, store the individual labels too: Chapter 10 needs them to measure how a judge compares with a typical human, not just with the adjudicated label.

A judge cannot be more reliable than its reference

If two humans agree at κ = 0.55, a judge that agrees with one of them at κ = 0.55 is as good as a second human, and asking for κ = 0.9 asks the judge to agree with that human's noise. Always measure human–human agreement first: it is the ceiling, and Chapter 10 compares judges against it.

3.5 · What labels cost, and where experts matter

Labels cost time, and time per item varies by two orders of magnitude: a binary tone label on a short reply takes 10–20 seconds; checking a refund answer against a policy table takes 1–2 minutes; checking a legal or medical answer needs a domain expert and can take 10 minutes or more. Three rules spend the budget well.

Double-label a sample, single-label the rest. Agreement only needs to be measured, not achieved on every item. Double-label 150–200 items to estimate κ with useful precision, and single-label the remainder once agreement is acceptable.

Put experts on the boundary. Generalist raters handle clear cases; route their disagreements and "unsure"s to an expert. Expert time goes where it changes labels.

Label the costly failure modes most carefully. A 2% labelling error on "tone" barely matters; a 2% error on "invented order status", where the bar is 0.2%, swamps the quantity you are measuring. Precision of labels should follow the cost structure of Chapter 1.

3.6 · The mental model to keep

Labels are measurements, and agreement is their precision

A label comes from a guideline applied by a person, and the guideline's boundary cases decide whether two people agree. Measure agreement against chance: κ = (po − pe)/(1 − pe), with raw agreement and label balance beside it so the rare-label paradox cannot mislead. Use alpha for real-world labelling with many raters and gaps, read pairwise numbers to find drift, and when disagreement survives a good guideline, keep the distribution. Human–human agreement is the ceiling every judge is measured against.

True agreement between two raters corresponds to κ = 0.6 at a 70% pass rate. You double-label 50 items to check it. Predict how wide the spread of measured kappas will be across repeated samples of 50, then simulate 2,000 samples and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. How many items would you double-label?

Solution

In 2,000 simulated samples of 50 items the middle 95% of measured kappas runs from 0.33 to 0.82, so one sample can easily read 0.4 ("moderate, fix the guideline") or 0.8 ("substantial, done"). 3.6Dawid–Skenestretch

Implement the binary Dawid–Skene EM algorithm: estimate each rater's sensitivity and specificity and the true label probabilities jointly. Simulate five raters with error rates between 5% and 30%, and show that the EM estimate of the true labels beats majority vote when one rater is much worse than the others.

Hint

E-step: for each item, the posterior that the true label is 1, from the prior and each rater's confusion probabilities. M-step: re-estimate each rater's sensitivity and specificity and the prior from those posteriors. Initialise with majority vote. Majority vote treats the bad rater's vote as equal; Dawid–Skene learns to discount it.
