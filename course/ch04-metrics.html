4 · Task Metrics, Proxy Metrics, and Cost

A metric is a function from one output to a number, and every metric rewards something slightly different from what you want. Exact match punishes a correct answer with a stray full stop; n-gram overlap gives a wrong date the same score as the right one, and a sentence that says the opposite of the reference a higher score than a correct paraphrase. This chapter measures those failures on concrete examples, then builds the metrics that hold up: checks that execute, schemas that validate, confidence that is calibrated, and cost and latency reported in the same table as quality.

The whole chapter in five sentences

Reference-based metrics compare an output with a stored answer: exact match needs normalisation, token F1 gives partial credit, and both work only when answers are short and canonical. N-gram overlap (BLEU, ROUGE) measures shared wording, not shared meaning: a wrong date scores like the right one and a negated sentence can outscore a correct paraphrase. Execution beats comparison whenever the output can be run or checked: unit tests for code, schemas for structured output, lookups for facts. If a system reports confidence, measure calibration (ECE and Brier), because routing and escalation decisions trust that number. Cost and latency are outcomes: report them per item beside quality and choose from the Pareto front.

4.1 · Reference-based metrics: exact match and F1

When a task has a short, canonical answer (a date, an amount, an entity, a label), the simplest metric is to compare the output with the stored answer. Done naively, it is wrong surprisingly often:

Output (gold: "The order ships on March 3.")raw EMnormalised EMtoken F1Correct?
the order ships on March 3011.00yes
It ships March 3rd.000.44yes
Your order will ship on 3 March000.67yes
The order ships on March 13.000.80no

Normalisation (lower-case, strip punctuation and articles, collapse whitespace; the SQuAD convention from Rajpurkar et al., 2016) rescues the first row. It cannot rescue the second and third, which are right but phrased differently. Token F1 gives partial credit for shared words, and that is its weakness as well as its strength: the wrong date in the last row scores 0.80, higher than two of the correct answers, because it shares every word but one.

The lesson is not "never use exact match". It is: parse first, then compare. If the answer is a date, extract a date from the output and compare dates. If it is an amount, extract the amount. A dedicated parser for the answer type turns all four rows into correct verdicts; a generic string metric gets at most two of them right.

Partial credit can reward the wrong answer

F1 and other overlap scores are averages over tokens, and a single wrong token (a digit, a "not", a name) can be the whole error. For any field where one token decides correctness, use a parsed exact comparison, not overlap.

Why did "The order ships on March 13." get a token F1 of 0.80 against the gold "The order ships on March 3."?
F1 ignores numbers
Normalisation removed the dates
It shares every word except the date, and F1 counts shared words equally
It is a near-paraphrase, so 0.80 is fair
Overlap is word-democratic. 4 of 5 normalised tokens match, so precision and recall are both 0.8. The one mismatched token is the entire meaning. Numbers are not ignored; they just count no more than "the".
What turns all four rows of the table into correct verdicts?
Extract a date from each output and compare it with the gold date
Stricter normalisation
Using F1 with a 0.5 threshold
Asking a judge model whether the answers match
Parse, then compare. A date parser maps "March 3", "March 3rd" and "3 March" to the same date and "March 13" to a different one. A judge could also work but is slower, costlier and less reliable than a parser for a well-typed field. Any F1 threshold that accepts row 2 (0.44) also accepts row 4 (0.80).
Where is normalised exact match the right metric?
Open-ended answers like summaries
Short answers from a closed set with one canonical spelling, such as a classification label
Any task, as long as normalisation is thorough
Code generation
Closed, canonical answer sets. A label like "billing" or "refund" has one form. Summaries have endless correct wordings, and code is better checked by running it (4.3).

4.2 · Why n-gram overlap fails for generation

BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) was designed for machine translation: the geometric mean of the n-gram precisions (n = 1 to 4) of a candidate against one or more references, times a brevity penalty. ROUGE (Lin, 2004) is its recall-oriented cousin for summaries; ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence. Both measure how much wording the output shares with a reference, which correlates with quality when outputs are constrained and references are many. For open-ended LLM outputs neither condition holds, and two examples show what goes wrong.

Worked example — negation beats a paraphrase
1
Reference: "You can return the item within 30 days for a full refund."
2
A correct paraphrase: "Returns are accepted for a full refund if you send it back within a month." BLEU 0.165, ROUGE-L 0.222, token F1 0.435.
3
The opposite claim: "You cannot return the item within 30 days for a full refund." BLEU 0.841, ROUGE-L 0.917, token F1 0.900.
4
Every overlap metric ranks the wrong answer far above the right one. The wrong answer reuses the reference's words; the right one says the same thing differently.

(BLEU here is sentence-level with add-one smoothing on the 2- to 4-gram precisions, as in Lin and Och, 2004; the exact values depend on the smoothing, the ranking does not.) Empirically, the correlation between BLEU or ROUGE and human judgements of open-ended generation is weak and often reverses at the top of the quality range, where systems differ in meaning rather than wording (Novikova et al., 2017, measured this across data-to-text systems). Use overlap metrics only for what they measure: closeness to a specific reference text, such as checking that a templated message still contains the required legal wording.

Embedding similarity has a milder version of the same problem

Cosine similarity between sentence embeddings (and metrics built on it, like BERTScore) handles paraphrases far better than BLEU, but sentence embeddings are notoriously weak at negation and numbers: "can return" and "cannot return" are close neighbours. Use them for retrieval and clustering (the Retrieval course), not as a correctness check.

Why does "You cannot return the item within 30 days" score BLEU 0.84 against "You can return the item within 30 days"?
BLEU has a bug with contractions
The brevity penalty rewards it
BLEU is computed on characters
It shares almost every n-gram with the reference; one changed word cannot outweigh that
Overlap measures wording. Only the n-grams that touch "cannot" fail to match, so precision stays high at every n. Brevity is not involved (the lengths are equal).
When is a reference-overlap metric a reasonable check?
For judging whether a summary is accurate
For confirming that required fixed wording (a legal disclaimer) is present
For ranking two chat models
For any task with at least four references
When wording is the requirement. Overlap is a fine detector of "the exact text is there". More references help translation-style tasks, but open-ended answers have too many valid forms.
Why are embedding-based similarity metrics weak correctness checks?
Sentence embeddings place negations and different numbers close together, so contradictory outputs can look similar
They are too slow
They cannot handle paraphrases
They only work in English
Close in meaning-space is not the same claim. Embeddings handle paraphrase well, which is why they beat BLEU on wording variation, but they are not built to flip on a single "not" or digit.

4.3 · Execution-based metrics: run it or check it

The most reliable metrics do not compare text at all. They take the output and do something with it that has an unambiguous result.

Output typeExecution checkWhat it catches that text comparison cannot
Coderun hidden unit tests in a sandbox (HumanEval-style pass@k, Transformers Ch 26)any correct implementation passes, whatever its wording
SQLexecute against a test database and compare result setsdifferent but equivalent queries
JSON / tool callsvalidate against a JSON Schema; check argument values against the recordmissing fields, wrong types, invented IDs
Factual claims about recordslook the fact up (order status, price, policy) and compareplausible but false statements
Math and exact answersparse the final answer, check symbolic equivalence"0.5", "1/2" and "50%" as one answer
Formats and constraintsprograms: "under 100 words", "no URLs", "contains a next step"instruction-following failures (IFEval-style)

Execution checks have their own failure modes, and they are worth knowing because they are silent. Weak tests pass wrong code (a function that returns a constant can pass a single test case). Flaky environments fail correct outputs (network timeouts, a package version). Over-strict schemas reject valid outputs (an optional field the schema forgot). For each check, keep a handful of known-good and known-bad outputs and run them on every change to the checker: a checker is code, and it has bugs.

Where execution checks are used in 2026

Execution-based evaluation now drives both evaluation and training: SWE-bench Verified scores coding agents by the repository's own tests, and the same kind of verifier provides the reward in RL with verifiable rewards (Transformers Ch 25, Post-Training Part IV). A check good enough to train on is held to a higher standard than one used only to report a number, because the model will find every gap in it.

4.4 · Calibration: when the system reports a confidence

Many systems attach a confidence to an output: a classifier's probability, a "confidence: high" field the model writes, the probability of the answer token, a retrieval score. Downstream logic trusts that number: "escalate to a human below 0.7", "show the answer only above 0.9". If 0.9 does not mean 90% right, those thresholds are wrong.

Calibration asks exactly that. Group outputs by stated confidence, and in each group compare the average confidence with the actual accuracy. The expected calibration error (ECE) is the item-weighted average gap over 10 equal-width bins; the Brier score is the mean squared difference between confidence and the 0/1 outcome, and combines calibration with how sharp the confidences are (Brier, 1950).

Worked example — the same accuracy, three calibrations
1
A simulated system: each output's true chance of being right is drawn uniformly between 50% and 100%, so its accuracy is 74.5% over 5,000 outputs. It reports confidence = true chance + an overconfidence offset (capped at 100%).
2
No offset: mean stated confidence 74.9%, ECE 1.7 points (pure sampling noise), Brier 0.169.
3
+10 points: mean confidence 84.0%, ECE 9.5 points, Brier 0.178.
4
+20 points: mean confidence 91.0%, ECE 16.5 points, Brier 0.199. Accuracy is identical in all three; only the trustworthiness of the confidence changed, and an "only show above 0.9" rule would show far more wrong answers than it promises.

Language models are often overconfident when they verbalise confidence ("I am 95% sure") and better calibrated in their token probabilities, at least before preference tuning; RLHF tends to worsen token-level calibration (the GPT-4 technical report showed this on MMLU, OpenAI 2023). Whatever source of confidence your system uses, measure it on your golden set before any threshold depends on it, and recalibrate if needed: fitting a single temperature to the logits on held-out data (Guo et al., 2017) or a monotone map from stated to empirical accuracy fixes most of the gap.

A code-generation eval passes 92% of outputs. You discover each task has a single test case. What is the risk?
The pass rate is too low
Execution-based evaluation does not work for code
Weak tests pass incorrect code, so 92% overstates correctness
The sandbox may be insecure
A checker is only as strong as its tests. A function that special-cases the one tested input passes. Add tests covering edge cases (EvalPlus showed that adding tests to HumanEval lowered reported pass rates noticeably). Sandbox security matters too, but it does not inflate the number.
Two systems have the same accuracy. System A has ECE 2 points, System B has ECE 15 points. When does the difference matter?
Whenever a decision (escalation, abstention, showing the answer) is made by thresholding the stated confidence
Never, since accuracy is the same
Only for multiple-choice tasks
Only when the Brier scores also differ
Calibration matters exactly when confidence drives an action. With B, a 0.9 threshold lets through far more wrong answers than expected. If nothing reads the confidence, calibration is irrelevant.
What does the Brier score capture that ECE does not?
Nothing; they are the same quantity
Only the accuracy
Whether the system is overconfident or underconfident
How sharp (decisive) the confidences are, as well as how calibrated
Brier = calibration + refinement. A system that always says "74.5%" is perfectly calibrated overall (ECE near 0) but useless for routing; its Brier score is worse than a calibrated system with sharp confidences. ECE alone does not say which way the miscalibration goes either; the reliability diagram does.

4.5 · Cost and latency are outcomes too

A quality number without its price is half a result. Every evaluation run already processes the golden set, so it costs nothing to record tokens in and out, dollars, and latency per item, and to report them in the same table as quality. The decision that matters is rarely "which is best?" but "which is best for what we can spend?".

Worked example — five configurations, one Pareto front
1
Five configurations of the support assistant, measured on the same golden set (quality = pass rate on the drivers; cost = dollars per conversation): large 91% at $0.0140; medium 88% at $0.0042; small 79% at $0.0009; medium with a retry on schema failure 90% at $0.0061; last year's large model 87% at $0.0150.
2
The Pareto front (no other configuration is at least as good and at least as cheap, and strictly better on one): small, medium, medium + retry, large. Last year's large model is dominated: medium is better and cheaper.
3
The choice along the front is a business decision with numbers attached: going from medium + retry to large buys 1 point of pass rate for 2.3× the cost per conversation. At a million conversations a month that is $7,900 for about 10,000 more passes.

Report per-item cost and latency with their distributions, not just means: a configuration whose p95 latency is three times its median will breach a latency SLO long before its mean suggests. And remember that the quality numbers on the front have error bars (Chapter 5); two points 1 point apart on a 400-item set are not distinguishable, and the cheaper one should win the tie.

Why is last year's large model (87% at $0.0150) not on the Pareto front?
Its quality is below 90%
Medium (88% at $0.0042) is both better and cheaper, so it dominates
It is the most expensive
Old models are excluded by definition
Dominated means beaten on both axes. Being expensive alone does not exclude a point (large is the most expensive and is on the front, because nothing matches its quality).
Medium + retry scores 90% and large 91% on a 400-item golden set. What is the honest recommendation?
Large, because 91 > 90
Medium + retry, because cost always matters more than quality
The 1-point gap is well inside the noise of 400 items, so the cheaper option wins unless a paired comparison shows a real difference
Run both in production forever
Ties go to the cheaper option. On 400 items each rate is ±3 points; a 1-point gap needs the paired test of Chapter 6 before it justifies 2.3× the cost.
Why record p95 latency and not just mean latency per configuration?
SLOs and user experience are set by the tail, and two configurations with the same mean can have very different tails
Means cannot be computed for latency
p95 is cheaper to measure
Because the mean is always larger
Users feel the tail. Long generations, retries and provider variance create heavy tails; the Inference course measures them in detail.

4.6 · The mental model to keep

Check meaning, not wording; check by doing, not by comparing

String comparison works only for short canonical answers, and then only after parsing the answer type. Overlap metrics measure shared wording, so they reward a negated copy of the reference over a correct paraphrase; use them only when wording is the requirement. Wherever an output can be executed, validated or looked up, do that. When a system reports confidence that drives a decision, measure its calibration. And put cost and latency in the same table as quality, so the choice is made along the Pareto front, with error bars deciding the ties.

4.1Write the parser, beat the metricimplementation

Write a date-and-amount extractor that turns an answer into a normalised (date, amount) pair, and use it to score the four rows of the 4.1 table. Then write ten more outputs that fool token F1 (a wrong answer scoring above 0.7, or a right one below 0.5) but not your parser.

Hint

Wrong numbers, wrong units, swapped day and month, and negations fool F1; ordinal suffixes, reordered phrases and synonyms ("a month" for "30 days" needs a rule) fool exact match. A parser must decide what it does with "a month": make that decision explicit and test it.

4.2Predict, then measure: BLEU against meaningpredict then measure

Take five policy statements. For each, write a correct paraphrase and a minimal edit that flips the meaning (a "not", a number, a swapped noun). Predict for how many of the five BLEU ranks the wrong edit above the paraphrase, then compute it with sentence_bleu, and do the same with ROUGE-L and token F1.

Solution

Expect all five, or close to it, for all three metrics: a minimal edit preserves nearly every n-gram, and a real paraphrase preserves few. The exercise is meant to make the result feel inevitable rather than surprising.

4.3Calibrate a thresholdessential

Using simulate_confidences(5000, 0.2), the policy is "show the answer if confidence ≥ 0.9, else escalate". (a) What fraction of shown answers are wrong? (b) Fit a recalibration map by binning (map each 0.1-wide bin's confidence to that bin's accuracy on half the data) and re-apply the policy on the other half. How do the escalation rate and the error rate among shown answers change?

Solution

(a) With 20 points of overconfidence, items stated at 0.9 or above have true chances from 0.7 upward. In the notebook 3,015 of 5,000 answers are shown, their stated confidence averages 0.98 (a promised error rate of about 2%), and 14.8% of them are wrong: eight times the promise. (b) After recalibration the top bin's measured accuracy is 86%, so no answer clears 0.9 and the policy escalates everything. That is the honest result: this system cannot deliver answers that are right 90% of the time at any threshold. The options are to lower the bar to what the system can meet (about 0.85 here) or to improve the system, and before recalibration nobody could see that choice existed.

4.4Price the frontsystems estimate

Per conversation: large uses 1,200 input and 300 output tokens at $3 and $15 per million; small uses the same tokens at $0.25 and $1.25. (a) Compute both costs. (b) A cascade answers with small and escalates to large when small's verified answer fails a check, which happens on 22% of conversations. What does the cascade cost per conversation, ignoring the check's own cost? (c) If the cascade's pass rate equals large's, what is the monthly saving at one million conversations?

Solution

(a) Large: 1,200 × 3/10⁶ + 300 × 15/10⁶ = $0.0081; small: $0.000675. (b) Small always runs, large on 22%: 0.000675 + 0.22 × 0.0081 = $0.00246. (c) (0.0081 − 0.00246) × 10⁶ ≈ $5,640 a month, a 70% cut, if and only if the check that decides escalation is reliable, which is a Chapter 10 question.
